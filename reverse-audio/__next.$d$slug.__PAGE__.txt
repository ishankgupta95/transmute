29:T6c8,Reversing audio is the one effect that costs nothing at all in itself. The file is decoded to raw samples, each channel is walked from the last sample to the first into a new buffer of exactly the same shape, and that is the whole operation — no filter, no resampling, no interpolation, nothing that has to make a decision about the sound. Every sample that was in the file is still in the file, and the length comes out identical to what went in. The channel count and sample rate do too on the WAV and FLAC arms; MP3 is the exception, because it carries at most two channels and 48 kHz, so a surround or hi-res source is folded or resampled on the way out and the run says so. What it is used for is mostly play and mostly deliberate: reversed cymbals and risers, backwards-speech effects, the reversed-then-re-reversed reverb trick, checking whether a recording really does say the thing somebody claims it says. The cost, when there is one, lives entirely in the format you export to. WAV writes the reversed samples uncompressed at 16 bits, so nothing is thrown away, though a 24-bit source is still reduced to 16-bit on the way out. FLAC compresses them without discarding any, at roughly half the size of WAV, and is the export that costs nothing beyond the decode. MP3 is a fresh lossy encode: reversing a lossy source and exporting it as MP3 means the audio has been through two lossy encoders, which is worth knowing rather than worth avoiding at all costs. The reverse itself runs on the Web Audio API in this tab; a container your browser will not decode, and FLAC output, fall back to FFmpeg compiled to WebAssembly, which is downloaded only if one of those actually happens.
