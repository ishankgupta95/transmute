4e:Tb93,Three things go wrong with subtitle files, and this page fixes all three. The first is the format: a player wants WebVTT and you have an SRT, or a site wants an SRT and someone sent you an ASS. The second is synchronisation — the words arrive two seconds before the mouth moves — which is a fixed offset applied to every cue. The third looks like the second and is not: a file timed against a 23.976 fps transfer and played at 25 drifts further out the longer it runs, because the error grows with elapsed time instead of staying constant. That one is fixed by re-timing between the two rates, which multiplies every cue time by timed-for over playing-at; getting the ratio the wrong way up doubles the drift rather than removing it, which is why the two rates are picked separately rather than guessed. The formats differ in small mechanical ways that break parsers. SRT writes its milliseconds after a comma and WebVTT after a full stop; WebVTT will not load in a browser without a WEBVTT line at the top; ASS stores hundredths of a second rather than thousandths, so writing ASS rounds outward, starts down and ends up, by up to 10 ms. All of that is handled. What cannot be handled is the other direction. ASS is a different model altogether — named styles, fonts and colours, on-screen positioning, karaoke timing inside the text itself — and converting one to SRT keeps the words and the times and discards every bit of the rest. The count of what went is printed rather than hidden: the cues that were vector drawings instead of words, the markup that had nowhere to go. Writing ASS produces a valid file with a single Default style; it is not a styled one. Encoding is the other half of the job. Subtitle files in the wild are frequently not UTF-8 — an older SRT is very often Windows-1252 — and reading one of those as UTF-8 with a forgiving decoder puts a replacement mark where every accent was, which is a file that looks converted and is quietly ruined. So the encoding is worked out rather than assumed: a byte-order mark is believed, BOM-less UTF-16 is spotted by its zero bytes, and the UTF-8 test is run strictly so that a file which is not UTF-8 fails it instead of coming back full of diamonds. Bytes that fail are read as Windows-1252 and labelled a guess, because a Cyrillic or Greek file is equally valid non-UTF-8, and a picker lets you name the codepage yourself when the guess is wrong. What gets written out is always UTF-8. The last of it is arithmetic on the timings. A negative shift that would start a cue before zero has that start pulled to 00:00:00; a cue that ends before zero is dropped rather than written out invalid; cues are sorted by start time and the SRT and WebVTT output is renumbered from 1; and overlapping cues are left exactly as they were, because two people talking at once is legal.
