FLAC is lossless compression: it models the waveform, stores the difference, and reproduces the original samples exactly when decoded. Run a WAV through FLAC and back and the result is bit-for-bit identical to what you started with — the same thing zipping a file does, but designed for audio and understood by audio software. The saving is real. Typical music compresses to 40 to 60 percent of its WAV size, which is why FLAC is the format of choice for archiving a CD collection, storing session masters, distributing live recordings and moving audio between studios without shipping gigabytes. Speech and quiet material often compress further; dense, loud, heavily limited material compresses less. Nothing about the conversion changes the sample rate, the bit depth or the channel layout, so a 96 kHz 24-bit master stays a 96 kHz 24-bit master. The trade-off is compatibility rather than quality: FLAC is well supported on desktop software, media servers and Android, and poorly supported in older car stereos and some Apple software, so it is an archive format rather than a delivery one.
