You have a document you would rather not have read by whoever the email gets forwarded to. A signed contract, a payslip, a scan of a passport, a board pack. So you go looking for a way to put a password on it, and within about forty seconds you are on a page that wants you to upload the file.

Stop and look at what that transaction actually is. To protect a confidential document, you are being asked to hand a stranger's server two things in the same request: the document, and the password you intend to protect it with. Whatever happens after that, for the duration of that request both halves of the secret exist together on someone else's machine. It is a strange shape for a privacy feature, and it is worth knowing that it is not the only shape available.

This piece is about what a PDF password actually is — the two kinds, what the encryption buys you, what the "restrict printing" checkbox is really doing, and the specific ways this goes wrong. Some of it is unflattering to the feature. All of it is worth knowing before you rely on it.

## There are two passwords, and you probably mean one of them

PDF defines two passwords, and almost every confusing thing about PDF security comes from people meeting the second one without being told it exists.

The **user password** is the one you mean. It is what a reader prompts for when the document opens. Without it, the file is ciphertext and nothing renders.

The **owner password** is different. It does not gate opening the document at all. It gates *changing the security settings* — it is the password a tool has to hold to alter or remove the protection. A file can carry an owner password and no user password, and that file opens for anyone, immediately, with no prompt. It just carries a note about what the reader is supposed to allow.

That combination is extremely common, and it is why you have almost certainly met a PDF that opened instantly and then refused to let you print it, or greyed out copy, or would not let you fill in the form. That file was never locked. It was labelled.

One practical detail, because it surprises people the first time: a PDF encrypted at 256 bits will not accept a user password with no owner password behind it. The format wants both. Tools that do not mention this quietly set the owner password to the same string you typed, which is almost always what you wanted. [Protect PDF](/protect-pdf) does the same thing and says so, and lets you set a separate owner password if you actually want the two to differ.

## What the encryption is, and what to pick

Modern PDF encryption is AES. There are two revisions worth caring about:

- **AES-256** is the current one, and is what you should use unless something forces your hand. It is what [Protect PDF](/protect-pdf) writes by default.
- **AES-128** is the older one. It exists for readers that predate AES-256 support — think a viewer from before Acrobat X. If the file has to open on some fixed-function device or an ancient corporate install, this is your fallback.

The format also still permits **RC4**, at 40 and 128 bits. RC4 is broken; 40-bit RC4 is trivially breakable with commodity hardware and has been for two decades. There is no honest reason to write it in 2026, and Protect PDF will not, at any setting. If a tool offers you "40-bit compatibility mode", it is offering you a document with a password painted on the outside.

What AES-256 buys you is real: without the password, the content is not recoverable. That is a genuinely strong guarantee, and it is the reason the next section is worth reading carefully — because everything *else* PDF calls "security" is not like this at all.

## Permission flags are advisory. That is not a caveat, it is the design

Here is the part that most pages selling this feature leave out.

When you tick "don't allow printing" or "don't allow copying", nothing is encrypted differently. What happens is that a number is written into the document's encryption dictionary, and specific bits of that number mean *the author would prefer you not print this*. A conforming reader looks at those bits and greys out the menu item. That is the entire mechanism.

Nothing enforces it. A reader that does not consult those bits will print the document without complaint. Any tool that can open the file can rewrite the number. And for a restrictions-only document — one with an owner password and no user password, the extremely common case — a tool does not even need a password to do it, because the document opens for everyone by definition.

You do not have to take this on trust. This site ships the demonstration: drop a restrictions-only PDF into [Unlock PDF](/unlock-pdf) and the restrictions come off without you typing anything, because there was nothing to type. That is not a clever exploit. It is what those flags are.

We would rather say this than sell you the opposite, so [PDF Permissions](/pdf-permissions) leads with it. And having said it, the flags are still genuinely useful — for the ordinary case, which is most cases. They tell colleagues, and more importantly their software, that this copy is the read-only one: that the printable version lives elsewhere, that the figures are not to be lifted into a deck, that this is not the file to edit. They stop accidents. They do not stop adversaries, and a page that implies otherwise is lying to you about a document you are trusting.

One deliberate absence worth mentioning: PDF also defines an accessibility-extraction flag, and Transmute does not offer a control for it. At the encryption revision these tools write, setting that flag leaves the permission bits byte-for-byte identical — it does nothing. A checkbox that appears to restrict something and does not is worse than no checkbox, so there isn't one.

## The 127-byte trap

This one is worth a paragraph on its own because it is silent, permanent, and nothing warns you.

A PDF password is capped at 127 bytes. Not characters — **bytes**, in UTF-8, so an accented or CJK or emoji password hits the ceiling far sooner than an ASCII one does. Go past it and the encryption still succeeds. The tool reports success. No warning is printed. And then that password will never open the file again: not the string you typed, not any truncation of it. The document is permanently unopenable and nothing told you.

[Protect PDF](/protect-pdf) refuses a password over the limit before it touches the document, which is not a nicety — it is the difference between an error message and a file you have lost.

While we are on the subject of things that cannot be undone: there is no reset. If you lose the password, the document is gone. That is what encryption is for, and it is precisely why no tool on this site can open a file whose password you do not know. Which brings us to the query this article is quietly answering.

## No, nothing here recovers a forgotten password

If you are here because you cannot open a file you own, the honest answer is that this site has nothing for you. [Unlock PDF](/unlock-pdf) removes a password you already know. It does not guess, does not run a dictionary, does not queue your file and email you a result. That is a deliberate line, and it is worth understanding why it holds: a correctly chosen AES-256 password is not recoverable by searching, and anything advertising otherwise is either finding a weak password, exploiting a weak scheme like the RC4 modes above, or taking your document.

What Unlock PDF is genuinely good for is the two cases people actually have. You know the password and want a copy without it — because you need to merge it, or search it, or send it somewhere that cannot prompt. Or the file opens fine and just refuses to print, in which case there is no password to know.

## Some files are not locked, they are broken

A related confusion is worth heading off: a PDF that will not open is not necessarily protected. A far more common cause is a damaged cross-reference table — the index that says where in the file each object lives. Interrupt a download, crash a program mid-save, run the file through something that miscounts, and readers start refusing it with a message about a damaged document.

That is a different problem with a different fix. [Repair PDF](/repair-pdf) re-parses the file, finds the objects by scanning when the index cannot be trusted, and writes out a fresh document with the table rebuilt. What it rebuilds is the index, not the contents — a truncated download is missing bytes and no tool can invent them — and when it finds nothing wrong it says so rather than claiming a rescue.

## Why doing this in the tab is a different transaction

All four of these tools run [`qpdf`](https://github.com/qpdf/qpdf), the Apache-2.0 PDF command-line tool and library, compiled to WebAssembly and executed inside your browser tab. The document is read from your disk into the tab's memory, qpdf operates on it there, and the result is offered back as a download. There is no upload step, because there is no server.

For most tools on this site that is a privacy nicety. Here it is a category difference, and it is worth being precise about why. With a server-based protect tool, the confidential document and the password that opens it are transmitted together, and for the length of that request they sit together on hardware you do not control. With a server-based unlock tool it is the encrypted document and its key, in the same request. Those are not "privacy inconveniences" — they are a disclosure of both halves of the secret to a third party, and no retention policy changes what has already been transmitted.

You do not have to believe any of this. Open your browser's developer tools, switch to the Network tab, drop a file into any of these pages and run it. There should be no request carrying your file. [Why browser-based tools are more private](/blog/why-browser-based-tools-are-private) walks through that check in more detail, and it applies to every tool here — the [PDF compressor](/compress-pdf), [merge](/merge-pdf), [split](/split-pdf), [text extraction](/pdf-to-text) and the rest.

## The short version

A **user password** encrypts the document; without it, nobody reads the file. Use AES-256, never RC4, and keep the password somewhere, because losing it means losing the document.

An **owner password** plus permission flags is a different thing entirely: a note about what the author would prefer, honoured by cooperative software and by nothing else. Set it to prevent accidents. Never set it believing it prevents anything.

And whichever you are doing, there is no reason for a confidential document to leave your machine to have a password put on it. [Add one](/protect-pdf), [take one off](/unlock-pdf), [set the flags](/pdf-permissions), or [rebuild a damaged file](/repair-pdf) — all of it happens in the tab, with nothing uploaded.
