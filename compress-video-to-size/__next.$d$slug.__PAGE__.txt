2e:T9c7,Every upload box has a number on it. Email attachments stop at about 25 MB, WhatsApp at 16 MB, a Discord message without Nitro at 10 MB — and a phone recording sails past all three within a minute. This page takes the number as the input. Pick 25, 16 or 10 MB, or type your own, and the compressor works backwards: it measures how long the clip runs, subtracts what the audio track will cost, leaves an allowance for the container's own bookkeeping, and divides the rest across the duration to get a video bitrate. It then picks the largest frame height that bitrate can honestly carry — spending few bits on many pixels is how a target gets met and a video gets destroyed — and encodes once. It will not spend a ceiling it does not need, either: aim 25 MB at a 4 MB clip and the bitrate is solved against what that clip already spends rather than against the 25 MB. What it cannot hand back is the clip itself. There is no pass-through here — every run is a fresh WebCodecs encode — so a file that already fits can come back no smaller, and when it does the page says so and tells you to keep your original. Nor can it promise the number. A video encoder is handed a bitrate, not a file size, and how close the finished file lands depends on the footage — a static talking head undershoots, a handheld pan through leaves overshoots, and no solver can know which you have before it encodes it. So the result is reported as what it is: the size actually produced, next to the size you asked for, with a plain statement when the target was not met rather than a file quietly larger than your limit. There is a floor as well, and it matters. Below a certain bitrate H.264 stops looking like your video and starts looking like blocks, so the solver will not go under it; a forty-minute recording asked to fit in 10 MB will come back at the floor, above your target, and say so. Trimming the clip first is the honest answer to that, and the video trimmer is one page over. The encoding runs on WebCodecs — the browser's own H.264 encoder, hardware-accelerated where the machine offers it — and mp4-muxer writes the MP4. That is the same path the main video compressor uses; the difference here is only which end of the arithmetic you supply. It also means this page needs a browser with WebCodecs, which today is Chrome and Edge: Safari and Firefox are told so up front rather than part-way through an encode.
