29:T55b,OGG is a container, and the audio inside it is usually Vorbis — a lossy codec that was, for years, the free alternative to MP3 and AAC. It is still what a lot of game assets, older podcast feeds, Wikipedia audio, Bandcamp downloads and Linux desktop recordings arrive as. The problem is not quality; it is that Ogg Vorbis never won the compatibility argument. Plenty of car stereos, older iPods, DJ software, television media players and upload forms simply refuse the extension. Converting to MP3 fixes that, and this tool does it by decoding the Vorbis stream and encoding a fresh MP3 from the decoded samples. It is worth understanding what that costs. Vorbis already threw away the parts of the signal its model considered inaudible; MP3 now throws away the parts of what is left that its own, different model considers inaudible. That is generational loss, and it is why the two formats being roughly comparable in quality does not mean the conversion is free. There is no way to avoid it — MP3 cannot hold a Vorbis stream, so a container swap is not on the table — but you can keep it small by choosing 256 or 320 kbps, which gives the encoder enough room that the second generation stays inaudible for most listeners. Where the browser cannot decode Ogg itself, as on Safari, an FFmpeg engine served from this site takes over.
