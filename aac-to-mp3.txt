45:T52b,AAC is the codec that replaced MP3 nearly everywhere it mattered: it is what YouTube streams, what Apple Music sells, what a phone records video sound with, and what most broadcast and streaming pipelines carry. It usually arrives wrapped in an M4A or MP4 container, but it also travels raw, as an ADTS stream in a bare .aac file — the shape a lot of stream rippers, IPTV recorders and DAB radio captures produce. Both are accepted here. The reason to convert at all is compatibility rather than quality. AAC beats MP3 at the same bitrate, so the change is a downgrade on paper; what you get in exchange is a file that older car stereos, cheap players, editing software and upload forms will accept without argument. The conversion is a full decode and re-encode, and both formats are lossy, which makes the output a second generation of compression rather than a copy. Choose 256 or 320 kbps if the source is music and you want that second generation to stay inaudible; 128 kbps is plenty for speech, where the difference is hard to hear at any setting. Most browsers decode AAC natively through the Web Audio API. Where one will not — Firefox on some systems has no AAC decoder at all — the tool falls back to an FFmpeg engine served from this site, and you get the same MP3 out.
