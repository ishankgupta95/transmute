Most image editing is not editing. It is one operation, done once, to one file, because something downstream will not accept the file as it is. The photo is sideways. The screenshot needs a border so it does not bleed into a white slide. The invoice has an account number on it that has to go before it is forwarded. None of that needs layers, or a history palette, or a subscription — it needs a single geometric change and a save button.

The gap between that job and the software normally recommended for it is enormous, and it is why the same handful of operations keep reappearing as separate little tools. What follows is what each of those operations actually does to your pixels, which of them cost you something, and the one caveat that applies to every single one.

## They are all the same operation underneath

Rotating, flipping, cropping to a circle, adding a border, stamping a watermark, pixelating a region, cutting a picture into a grid — every one of these is the same three steps.

The file is **decoded** into a raster: a rectangular grid of pixel values in memory. A new canvas is sized for whatever the result needs — the same size for a flip, a swapped width and height for a quarter turn, a larger one for a border. The source is **drawn** onto it, sometimes transformed, sometimes masked. And the canvas is **encoded** back into a file.

That is the whole mechanism, and knowing it answers most of the questions people bring to these tools. It tells you why a flip cannot change the dimensions and a rotation can. It tells you why a border is exact arithmetic rather than a resize. And it tells you the thing almost nobody mentions, which is worth its own section.

## Every edit is a re-encode, and on a JPEG that costs you

The decode-draw-encode cycle ends with an encoder. Which encoder, and what it does, is the difference between an operation that costs nothing and one that costs a little every time.

If the output is **PNG or WebP in its lossless mode**, the encode preserves the pixel values exactly. Turn a PNG ninety degrees and you get the same pixels in a different arrangement, byte-for-byte faithful to what came out of the decoder.

If the output is **JPG**, the encoder throws detail away, the same way it did when the file was first made. Take a photo out of your camera, rotate it, save it as JPG, add a border, save it as JPG again, and you have paid twice. Each individual step is very likely invisible. The stacking is what gets you, and it is why doing three operations in one session is worse than deciding what you want and doing it once.

Two consequences worth acting on:

- **Do the edits you know you need in one pass**, rather than saving and re-opening between each one.
- **Do not "upgrade" a JPEG to PNG to protect it.** PNG stores exactly what it is given, including every artifact the JPEG already had, at several times the size. It stops further loss; it does not undo any.

There is a genuinely lossless way to turn a JPEG — rewriting the orientation tag in its metadata rather than the pixels — but it is a different operation from what these pages do, and no page here claims otherwise. A tool that advertises "lossless rotation" while redrawing on a canvas is describing something it is not doing.

## Your phone already rotated the photo, and that explains a lot

Here is the fact that resolves the single most confusing thing about rotating photos.

A phone camera does not turn its sensor when you turn the handset. It writes the pixels in whatever order the sensor produced them — frequently sideways — and adds a small metadata tag, EXIF Orientation, that says "display this rotated ninety degrees clockwise". Every modern viewer reads that tag and obeys it. So the picture you see is already the corrected one, and the pixels on disk are not.

Browsers do the same thing. By the time a page like [Rotate Image](/rotate-image) has a decoded raster to work with, the orientation tag has already been applied and discarded. So a turn you ask for is composed on top of the picture *as displayed*, which is exactly what you want: the preview, the dimension readout and the downloaded file all describe the same picture.

It also explains the classic failure elsewhere. Software that ignores the orientation tag shows you a sideways photo; software that applies it twice shows you an upside-down one. Both are metadata bugs rather than pixel bugs.

## Flipping is not rotating

They get confused constantly, and the difference is mechanical. A rotation turns the picture about its centre, and a quarter turn swaps width for height. A [flip](/flip-image) reflects it across an axis and moves no edge at all, so the dimensions are identical before and after.

Text is the giveaway. Rotated text is readable if you tilt your head. Flipped text is mirror writing.

The commonest real use is undoing a selfie. Front-facing cameras mirror the preview so that moving left moves your reflection left — but many of them save the unmirrored frame, which is the face everyone else sees and not the one you composed. A horizontal flip switches between those two versions. Neither is wrong; they are just different conventions about which one is "you".

Rotation has an orientation tag it can hide behind. A mirror does not: there is no metadata flag for reflection, so the pixels genuinely have to move.

## Borders are arithmetic, not a resize

[Adding a border](/add-border) is the least surprising operation on this list and the one people most often expect to be lossy. It is not. The canvas is made bigger, filled with the colour you picked, and your image is drawn into it whole at its original size. Nothing is resampled, nothing is scaled, and not one pixel of your picture is touched.

The arithmetic is worth stating because it is the only thing you need to predict the result: the new width is the old width plus left plus right, and the new height is the old height plus top plus bottom. An 800 × 600 picture with twenty pixels on every edge becomes 840 × 640.

Unlinking the edges is what makes the tool useful rather than decorative. A wide strip along the bottom is where a caption goes. An even margin on three sides with a deeper one at the bottom is the classic print mount, because a frame that is optically centred is measurably bottom-heavy.

One thing it deliberately will not do is give you a transparent margin. A border is a painted fill, and painting white is not the same as leaving the area empty — so the tool asks for a colour rather than pretending the two are interchangeable.

## What a watermark actually buys you

A [watermark](/watermark-image) is drawn into the pixels. That is its whole strength and its whole limitation.

The strength is that it survives everything metadata does not. Re-saving strips EXIF; screenshotting strips EXIF; uploading to almost any social platform strips EXIF. A visible mark drawn into the picture survives all three, because by then it *is* the picture.

The limitation is that anyone can crop it off, paint over it, or clone it out in about a minute. Corner watermarks are the easiest to remove and the least intrusive to look at; a tiled mark across the whole frame is genuinely awkward to remove because removing it means reconstructing the subject underneath. That is the real trade-off, and it is a trade-off about how much you are willing to spoil your own image.

So a watermark is a credit and a deterrent. It is not protection, and any tool that sells it as protection is selling you a feeling.

## Pixelation is not redaction

This is the section that matters most, and it is the one most tools skip.

[Pixelating](/pixelate-image) a region replaces it with large flat blocks. The original detail is genuinely gone from the file — it is not hidden in a layer, and nothing in the output recovers it. That sounds like enough, and for a face in a crowd it usually is.

It is not enough when the covered content comes from a small, known set of possibilities. If you pixelate a six-digit code, a number plate, a date of birth or a short account number, an attacker does not need to recover your pixels. They can generate every candidate value, render each one in a plausible font at your image's scale, pixelate it with the same block size, and compare. The correct answer produces a near-identical pattern of blocks. This has been demonstrated repeatedly against real redactions, and larger blocks raise the cost without changing the shape of the attack.

The rule that follows is simple and absolute: **if it must stay secret, crop it out or paint over it.** Removing the pixels is a different operation from averaging them, and only one of them is irreversible. Pixelation is for anonymising a face, softening a background, or obscuring something nobody is going to attack. It is not a redaction tool, and calling it one is how people get hurt.

## A circle is still a rectangle

Every raster image format stores a rectangle. There is no such thing as a circular PNG.

What [cropping to a circle](/crop-circle) produces is a square image whose corners are fully transparent, which reads as a circle anywhere transparency is honoured — a slide, a web page, an app that masks avatars — and as a square wherever it is not.

That is why the format choice is restricted on that page. JPG has nowhere to store transparency, so a JPEG "circle" is a circle painted onto a white square, and it will show as a white box the moment it lands on a dark background. PNG and WebP both carry an alpha channel, so those are the two the page offers rather than letting you pick the one that cannot work.

## Splitting a picture, and where the leftover pixels go

[Cutting an image into a grid](/image-splitter) is arithmetic again, and the interesting part is the remainder. A thousand pixels across three columns is not three tiles of 333 — that loses a pixel, and a lost column of pixels is a visible seam when the tiles are reassembled or posted side by side.

So the leftovers are handed out, one per tile, until they run out: 333, 334, 333. Two tile sizes on one axis, differing by one pixel, adding up to exactly the source width. The alternative approaches — padding the last tile, stretching it, or dropping the remainder — all produce something that is not your picture any more.

The other practical detail is the filenames. Tiles come out named by row and column, one-based, so a file browser sorting alphabetically puts them back in reading order. That is what you want when the tiles are going into a carousel in sequence, and it is not what you get from a tool that numbers them 1 to 9.

## The odd one out: an image that arrives as text

Sometimes the image is not a file at all. It is a wall of letters and digits inside a CSS rule, a JSON payload, an email source or a page you are debugging — an image encoded as Base64 text so it can travel where only text is allowed.

[Turning that back into a file](/base64-to-image) is mostly an exercise in accepting what people actually paste. A bare payload, a whole `data:` URI with its parameters, a block a terminal wrapped at 76 columns, the entire `<img>` tag copied out of a page's source. Whitespace has to be stripped before the length is checked, or the wrapping is counted as data and the padding comes out wrong. The URL-safe alphabet used by JWTs and many APIs swaps two characters and is rejected outright by the browser's own decoder until it is translated back.

And then there is the label. The `image/png` in front of a data URI is something a person typed, and it is wrong often enough that it should not be trusted: the honest move is to read the format out of the decoded bytes and name the download from those, saying so when the two disagree. A tool that believes the label hands you a `.png` containing JPEG.

## The one decision every edit shares

Whatever the operation, the last question is the same: what do you write out?

**PNG** stores every pixel exactly, keeps transparency, and is the right answer for screenshots, logos, line art, anything with flat colour and hard edges, and anything with a transparent area. Its weakness is photographs, where it can easily be several times larger than the JPEG you started with.

**JPG** is small, universally accepted, lossy, and has no alpha channel — transparent areas come out white. It is the right answer for photographs going somewhere old or somewhere fussy, and the wrong answer for a screenshot full of text.

**WebP** does both jobs: it carries an alpha channel and it is usually the smallest of the three at a comparable quality. It is read by every current browser, and it is the sensible default for anything headed to the web.

The one that catches people is starting from a JPEG and writing a PNG "to be safe". You get a much bigger file that has protected nothing, because the loss already happened. Match the source unless you have a reason not to — which is why these pages open on the format you dropped rather than always on PNG.

## The short version

Every one of these operations is a decode, a draw and an encode. That is why they are fast, why they can run entirely in a browser tab with nothing uploaded, and why the format you choose on the way out matters more than the operation itself.

Do the edits you need in one pass. Keep the original. Write PNG or WebP when transparency or sharp edges are involved, JPG when size and compatibility are, and do not expect any of them to give back what an earlier encoder already discarded.

And of the eight operations here, exactly one carries a risk rather than a cost. Pixelation looks like redaction and is not. If the thing under the blocks would matter to a stranger, [crop it out](/crop-image) instead.
