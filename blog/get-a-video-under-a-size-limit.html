How to Get a Video Under 25 MB

Email stops at 25 MB, WhatsApp at 16, Discord at 10. Here is the arithmetic that turns a size limit into a bitrate — and why the number is a target rather than a promise.

Email attachments stop at about 25 MB. WhatsApp will not take a video over 16 MB. Discord, without Nitro, stops at 10 MB. A phone recording clears all three inside a minute, which is why "compress this video" is nearly always shorthand for something much more specific: get this file under a number somebody else picked.

That is a different problem from "make this smaller", and it has a different shape. Making a video smaller is a judgement call about quality. Getting it under a number is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is worth understanding — partly because it tells you when the answer is going to be no.

One equation

A video file's size is very close to its duration multiplied by its bitrate. Bitrate is bits per second; duration is seconds; multiply them and you have bits, which you divide by eight for bytes. Everything else — the container headers, the index of where each frame starts, the metadata — is bookkeeping in the low single-digit percentages.

Read that equation backwards and the size limit becomes a bitrate:

bitrate = target bytes × 8 ÷ duration in seconds

Say you have a three-minute clip and Discord's 10 MB ceiling. Ten mebibytes is 10,485,760 bytes, which is 83,886,080 bits. Divide by 180 seconds and you get about 466 kbps for the whole file. That is the number an encoder needs to be told.

This is exactly what Compress Video to Size does. You give it the size, it measures how long the clip runs, and it solves for the bitrate. There is no magic in it, and the fact that there is no magic in it is the useful part: you can do the same sum yourself and know in advance roughly what you are asking for.

The audio takes a slice first

The 466 kbps in that example is the budget for the whole file, and the audio track is inside it. AAC at 64 kbps is thin but intelligible for speech; 96 kbps is comfortable; 128 kbps is what you would want for music. Subtract that before you hand the rest to the video encoder, or you will overshoot by exactly the amount you forgot.

So the three-minute Discord clip really has around 400 kbps for the picture. Now the question becomes whether 400 kbps is enough for what is in your video, and that depends entirely on what is in your video.

Why a target is never a guarantee

Here is the part most size-target tools are quiet about.

You cannot hand an encoder a file size. You hand it a bitrate, and it tries to average that over the clip. In a single pass it is working blind: it does not know that the calm establishing shot at the front is followed by ninety seconds of handheld motion, so it spends its early budget at the rate it was told and adjusts as it goes. The finished file lands near the target, not on it.

Which direction it misses depends on the footage:

  • Static content undershoots. A slide deck, a talking head against a plain wall, a screen recording of mostly-still text — there is little for the encoder to spend bits on, and it comes in under budget.
  • Constant motion overshoots. Handheld footage, water, foliage, confetti, a fast pan. Every frame differs from the last and the encoder cannot buy that back.

A tool that reports "done" without telling you the size it produced is hiding the interesting half of the result. The honest presentation is the one this site uses for its other size-target tools: show the size actually reached next to the size that was asked for, and say plainly when the second was not met.

The floor, and why trimming beats squeezing

Below a certain bitrate, H.264 stops looking like your video. Detail goes first, then the fine gradients band, then the picture breaks into visible blocks, and past that point you are shipping something nobody can watch — which was not the goal.

So a solver has to have a floor, and a floor means some requests are impossible. Ask forty minutes of footage to fit in 10 MB and the arithmetic asks for about 35 kbps. There is no encoder setting that makes that watchable. The result comes back at the floor, above your target, with a note saying so.

When you hit that, the lever is not bits. It is seconds. Halve the duration and you double the bitrate available to every remaining second, with no quality cost at all to the part you keep. Video Trimmer does that with a stream copy — it does not re-encode anything, so the trimmed clip is bit-for-bit the frames it kept — and then the compressor has a much easier sum to solve. For a long recording, trim first is almost always the right order.

The other lever is resolution. Bitrate is bits per second, but perceived quality is closer to bits per pixel: the same 400 kbps that looks poor at 1080p looks respectable at 480p, because there are a quarter as many pixels asking for a share. That is the trade the preset-based Compress Video makes for you — its WhatsApp preset caps height at 720p, its email preset at 480p — and it is why the two pages are worth having side by side. One asks you for a preset and hands you a size; the other asks you for a size and picks the settings.

Two things people forget to account for

Subtitles are not in the video. If you are shipping a clip with captions, the captions are almost certainly a separate .srt sitting next to it, and they cost nothing against your size limit — a feature-length subtitle file is tens of kilobytes. What does go wrong is the format: a player that wants WebVTT will not read an ASS file, and a file timed against a 23.976 fps transfer drifts steadily out of sync when played at 25. Subtitle Converter handles both, and it is worth checking before you re-encode the video, because re-encoding will not fix a timing problem that was never in the picture.

Sometimes you did not want the video. If the thing you are actually sending is a lecture, an interview or a podcast recording, the picture is the entire reason the file is too big. Extract the audio and a forty-minute recording that would not fit in 10 MB as video becomes a comfortable MP3 with room to spare. It is a blunt answer, but for a surprising share of "this file is too big to send" it is the correct one.

Where the encoding happens

Both compressors on this site run on WebCodecs — the browser's own H.264 encoder, hardware-accelerated where the machine provides it — with mp4-muxer assembling the MP4. That means the encoding happens in your tab and the video is never uploaded, and it also means the pages need Chrome or Edge. Both check the four encoder-side pieces they rely on — VideoEncoder, AudioEncoder, VideoFrame and requestVideoFrameCallback — before anything starts, and Firefox and Safari do not currently pass that check. Being told up front is better than being told part-way through a long encode.

None of this is complicated once the equation is in front of you. Duration times bitrate is size; a limit divided by duration is a bitrate; the audio comes out of the same budget; and the encoder aims rather than lands. A tool that says all four of those out loud is more useful than one that shows a spinner and hopes.
