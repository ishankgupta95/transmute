29:T50d,WAV is the format audio software expects. Digital audio workstations, mastering chains, forensic and transcription tools, sample libraries and broadcast delivery specs all assume uncompressed PCM, and several of them will refuse a compressed file outright rather than decode it for you. M4A is the opposite: it is what an iPhone voice memo, a GarageBand bounce, an iTunes purchase or a screen recording's audio track arrives as, and it holds AAC — compressed, lossy, and awkward to edit. Converting bridges the two. The tool decodes the M4A to raw samples and writes them into a standard 16-bit WAV at the file's own sample rate, which is what an editor wants to see. Two things are worth being clear about. The first is size: uncompressed audio runs about 10 MB per minute in stereo, so a 5 MB voice memo can land near 50 MB as a WAV. The second is quality. Decoding to WAV does not restore anything AAC discarded — that information is gone and no conversion brings it back. What the WAV gives you is a lossless container to work in, so that every edit, filter and bounce after this point costs nothing further. Most browsers decode AAC natively; where one will not, an FFmpeg engine served from this site does it instead.
